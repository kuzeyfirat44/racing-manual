Four Teachers

Meet the
Four Cars

Each car teaches a different lesson about how cars work. Figures are approximate and vary by year and market.

Subaru BRZ GT300

Super GT race car · rear-wheel drive

Often called the BRZ GT3. Strictly, it is a GT300-class car built to Japan's own rules that races FIA GT3 cars under Balance of Performance.

Engine
3.0 L flat-six twin-turbo from 2026 (the EJ20 turbo flat-four before that)
Power
Set by BoP. The road BRZ makes about 228 hp
Weight
Set by BoP (1,225 kg at one 2022 round). Road BRZ about 1,300 kg
Drive
Rear-wheel drive

What it teaches. Low engine, low centre of gravity. The boxer engine sits low and behind the front axle, so the car turns in cleanly.

Where it thrives. Corner entry, balance and long races where tyre care matters.

Mitsubishi Lancer Evolution III

1995-96 · turbo all-wheel drive

The Evo III is the third step in the road-going rally homologation line that built the Evo name.

Engine
2.0 L turbo inline-four (4G63)
Power
270 PS, about 266 hp (Japan's informal cap)
Weight
About 1,200 kg
Drive
All-wheel drive, 5-speed manual

What it teaches. Boost plus traction. A turbo makes big power on demand, and four driven wheels put it down in almost any weather.

Where it thrives. Wet, gravel and low-grip roads, and hard launches out of slow corners.

Mazda MX-5 (ND)

2015 onward · rear-wheel drive roadster

The best-selling two-seat sports car in history, and the clearest example of "light is right".

Engine
2.0 L naturally aspirated four (SKYACTIV-G)
Power
About 181 hp (varies by market)
Weight
About 1,050 kg
Drive
Rear-wheel drive, 6-speed manual

What it teaches. Light is right. Low weight and near 50/50 balance make every steering, brake and throttle input easy to feel.

Where it thrives. Learning car control, twisty roads and low running costs.

Honda Civic Type R (EK9)

1997-2000 · front-wheel drive hot hatch

Built by hand-finishing the engine, with a limited-slip differential and no sound deadening to save weight.

Engine
1.6 L naturally aspirated VTEC four (B16B)
Power
185 PS, about 182 hp, peaking at 8,200 rpm
Weight
About 1,050 kg
Drive
Front-wheel drive, 5-speed manual, helical LSD

What it teaches. Revs and gearing. A high-revving engine and short gears make speed from engine speed rather than boost.

Where it thrives. Mountain roads, track days and everyday use as a practical hatchback.

Start Here

Car
Basics

Seven ideas explain most of what a car does. Each one points back to one of the four cars so you can see it at work.

Engine

An engine burns fuel and air inside cylinders. Each burn pushes a piston, and the pistons turn the crankshaft. Displacement is the total cylinder volume, so a 2.0 L engine moves 2,000 cc of air per two crank turns, in theory. Burning more air and fuel per turn makes more power.

See it in: The MX-5 and EK9 both run this four-stroke cycle, but the EK9 spins it to 8,400 rpm.

Naturally aspirated or turbo

A naturally aspirated engine breathes at normal air pressure. A turbocharger uses exhaust gas to spin a compressor that forces extra air in, so the same engine burns more fuel and makes more power.

See it in: The Evo III is turbocharged. The MX-5 and EK9 are not.

Power and torque

Torque is twisting force at the crankshaft. Power is how quickly that work is done, which is torque multiplied by engine speed. Torque is the shove you feel pulling away; power decides how fast you can ultimately go.

See it in: High-revving cars like the EK9 make their power high in the rev range, so you work the gearbox more.

Drivetrain

Front-wheel drive sends power and steering through the same wheels. Rear-wheel drive splits the jobs: the front steers, the rear pushes. All-wheel drive spreads power across four tyres for traction.

See it in: EK9 is front-drive, BRZ and MX-5 are rear-drive, Evo III is all-wheel drive.

Gearbox and clutch

The clutch connects and disconnects the engine from the gearbox. The gearbox keeps the engine in its useful rev range. Short gear ratios give quick acceleration; tall ratios give relaxed cruising.

See it in: The EK9 uses short ratios so the engine stays up in its power band.

Tyres and grip

Four patches of rubber, each about the size of a hand, touch the road. Every force the car makes passes through them. Grip is shared: the more a tyre is braking or accelerating, the less it has left for cornering.

See it in: Race cars like the BRZ GT300 use wide slicks; road cars compromise for rain and longevity.

Brakes and weight transfer

Brakes turn motion into heat. Under braking, weight moves forward and loads the front tyres. Under power it moves back, and in a corner it moves to the outside. A lighter car has less to stop, turn and shift around.

See it in: The 1,050 kg MX-5 and EK9 feel agile partly because there is less mass to move.

VTEC

VTEC and variable valve timing

Honda's VTEC switches the camshaft to a more aggressive profile at high rpm. The engine is gentle at low revs and breathes deeply near the redline. In the EK9's B16B, the switch is why it feels like two engines in one.

TURBO

Boost, lag and the 4G63

A turbo needs exhaust flow to spin up, so there is a delay before boost arrives. Engineers shorten it with smaller turbines, twin-scroll housings or anti-lag. The Evo III's 4G63 is a classic of making big power from two litres.

LSD

Differentials

A differential lets the left and right wheels turn at different speeds in a corner. An open diff sends power to the wheel with the least grip. A limited-slip differential limits that, so the loaded wheel can still push. The EK9's helical LSD is why a front-wheel-drive car can pull out of corners. The Evo adds a centre differential to share power front to rear.

50/50

Weight distribution and polar moment

A 50/50 front-to-rear split, which Mazda targets with the MX-5, makes a car rotate predictably. Mass packed near the centre also changes direction more eagerly than mass at the ends, like a short broom handle compared with a long one.

CAMBER

Suspension geometry

Camber is the tilt of the wheel, toe is whether it points in or out, and caster is the angle of the steering axis. They keep the tyre flat on the road as the body rolls. Stiffer anti-roll bars at the front add understeer; stiffer at the rear add oversteer.

AERO

Aerodynamics

Downforce presses the car into the road, and grows with the square of speed. Drag costs top speed and fuel. The BRZ GT300 relies on wings and a diffuser that only work at speed. The MX-5 and EK9 depend almost entirely on mechanical grip.

BoP

Balance of Performance

Racing series level out different cars by changing weight, power or boost. That is how a GT300 BRZ can race GT3 cars in Super GT. See the Racing Manual's Super GT page.

Lancer Evo III

Runner-up: Toyota GR Yaris

Civic Type R EK9

Runner-up: Porsche 911 GT3

Beyond the Four

More Cars,
More Lessons

Six more cars that are the best at something, and what they give up for it.

Lotus Elise

Thrives at: Steering feel and lightness

A tiny, light mid-engine car, often under 800 kg. Hydraulic steering gives more feedback than almost anything built today.

Weaker at: Everyday comfort and storage space.

Porsche 911 GT3

Thrives at: Braking and high-rev traction

The rear-engine layout puts weight over the driven wheels, so it launches hard out of corners. Its naturally aspirated flat-six revs to about 9,000 rpm.

Weaker at: Price, and the cost of everything that wears out.

Nissan GT-R (R35)

Thrives at: Launching and straight-line speed

All-wheel drive, twin turbos and a clever launch system make it quick in a straight line. It is heavy, at about 1,750 kg.

Weaker at: Lightness, and feeling nimble in tight corners.

Toyota GR Yaris

Thrives at: Modern rally-bred all-wheel drive

A small turbocharged hatch with a rally-derived all-wheel-drive system. In spirit, it is the Evo's modern successor.

Weaker at: Rear-seat room and ride comfort.

Honda NSX (first generation)

Thrives at: Balance and everyday usability

A mid-engine layout with an aluminium body gave supercar balance with Honda reliability, easy visibility and a usable cabin.

Weaker at: Outright power against later rivals.

Tesla Model S Plaid

Thrives at: Acceleration

Electric motors deliver full torque instantly. Tesla claims about two seconds to 60 mph, though the car weighs over 2,100 kg.

Weaker at: Weight, so tyres and brakes struggle on a track.
