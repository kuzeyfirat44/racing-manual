International Motor Sports Association

The Birth of
American Endurance

Founded in 1969 by John Bishop, IMSA became the definitive arena for sports car racing in North America — a series defined by machinery pushed to its absolute limit, day and night.

Years of History
1969
IMSA Founded
John Bishop and NASCAR founder Bill France Sr. establish the International Motor Sports Association, creating a unified set of rules for North American sports car racing. The series quickly attracts support from major manufacturers.
1971–1977
Multi-Class Racing & The GTX Era
IMSA's GTO and GTU classes let very different cars race side by side. Multi-class racing becomes the series' greatest strength. In 1977 the GTX class opens the door to turbocharged silhouette racers, and the Porsche 935 becomes the car to beat.
1981–1993
The GTP Era & Peak Factory Warfare
The Grand Touring Prototype class turns IMSA into a battleground for Porsche, Jaguar, Nissan and Toyota, with purpose-built prototypes producing over 700bhp. The Porsche 962 wins the IMSA GTP title every year from 1985 to 1988 and wins the Daytona 24 Hours five times.
1994
World Sports Cars Replace GTP
With costs out of control, IMSA replaces GTP with cheaper open-top World Sports Car (WSC) prototypes. The Ferrari 333 SP and Riley & Scott Mk III become the stars of the new era.
1999–2013
ALMS vs Grand-Am
Don Panoz launches the American Le Mans Series in 1999 using Le Mans rules, carrying the IMSA name into the 21st century. A rival series, Grand-Am, starts in 2000 with the Daytona 24 Hours as its crown jewel. American sports car racing is split in two for 14 years.
2014
IMSA WeatherTech Championship Born
The merger of Grand-Am and the American Le Mans Series creates today's IMSA WeatherTech SportsCar Championship, with Prototype, Prototype Challenge, GTLM and GTD classes. The DPi formula follows in 2017.
2023
GTP Returns & The Hybrid Era
The famous GTP name comes back for a new top class of hybrid LMDh prototypes. Porsche, Cadillac, BMW and Acura all build factory cars, and the same machines can now race at both Daytona and Le Mans.

IndyCar is North America's premier single-seater series and the only championship that races on ovals, street circuits and road courses in a single season. Its heart is the Indianapolis 500, first run in 1911 and still the largest single-day sporting event in the world.

1909–1911
The Brickyard Opens & The First Indy 500
Carl Fisher builds the Indianapolis Motor Speedway in 1909 and resurfaces it with 3.2 million paving bricks, which gives the track its nickname. On 30 May 1911 Ray Harroun wins the first Indianapolis 500 in the Marmon Wasp, driving solo with a rear-view mirror instead of a riding mechanic.
1956
USAC Takes Control
After AAA withdraws from motorsport, the United States Auto Club (USAC) takes over the American national championship. The front-engine roadster era follows, with legends like A.J. Foyt and Parnelli Jones.
1965
The Rear-Engine Revolution
Jim Clark wins the Indy 500 in the rear-engined Lotus 38, the first rear-engined car to win the race. European F1 technology takes over and the front-engine roadster disappears almost overnight.
1979
CART Is Born
Team owners led by Roger Penske, Pat Patrick and Dan Gurney break away from USAC to form Championship Auto Racing Teams (CART). Through the 1980s and early 90s CART becomes a global rival to F1, attracting stars like Nigel Mansell, who wins the 1993 title as the reigning F1 World Champion.
1996–2007
The Split
Speedway owner Tony George founds the rival Indy Racing League (IRL), built around the Indy 500. American open-wheel racing is divided into two series (CART, later Champ Car, and the IRL). The split drains sponsors, fans and TV audiences for more than a decade, while NASCAR's popularity booms.
2008
Reunification
Champ Car is absorbed into the IRL and the two series finally become one again, later branded the IndyCar Series. For the first time in twelve years, every top American open-wheel team races for the same championship.
2012
The Dallara DW12
A new spec chassis debuts, named in honour of Dan Wheldon, the two-time Indy 500 winner who tested it before losing his life at Las Vegas in 2011. Chevrolet and Honda supply turbocharged V6 engines, a rivalry that continues today.
2020–2024
The Penske Era, Aeroscreen & Hybrid Power
Roger Penske buys IndyCar and the Indianapolis Motor Speedway. The Aeroscreen cockpit protection is introduced in 2020, and in July 2024 hybrid energy recovery arrives, adding a driver-deployed electric boost to the V6 engines.

Iconic Races

1911

The First Indianapolis 500

30 May 1911

Forty cars take the start of the International 500-Mile Sweepstakes on the brand-new brick surface. Ray Harroun's Marmon Wasp is the only single-seater in the field. Instead of carrying a riding mechanic to watch for traffic, Harroun fits a mirror, one of the first used on a car. He averages 74.6 mph and wins in just under seven hours, starting a tradition that is now more than a century old.

0.043

Unser Jr. vs Goodyear

1992 Indy 500

In one of the coldest Indy 500s ever, crashes eliminate many of the favourites. Al Unser Jr. holds off Scott Goodyear, who started last, by just 0.043 seconds, still the closest finish in Indianapolis 500 history. An emotional Unser Jr. tells the crowd: "You just don't know what Indy means."

2006

Hornish Snatches It at the Line

2006 Indy 500

19-year-old rookie Marco Andretti, grandson of Mario, looks certain to win after passing his own father Michael in the closing laps. On the final straight Sam Hornish Jr. slingshots out of his slipstream and wins by 0.0635 seconds. It is the first last-lap pass for the win in Indy 500 history, and the family's famous "Andretti Curse" at Indianapolis strikes again.

100

Wheldon's Final Victory

2011 Indy 500

At the 100th anniversary race, rookie J.R. Hildebrand leads into the very last corner and then hits the wall while passing a slower car. Dan Wheldon, driving a part-time entry, steers around the wreck to win the Indy 500 for the second time. It is one of the most dramatic finishes the Speedway has ever seen, and it became even more poignant when Wheldon was killed at Las Vegas five months later.

The Original World Championship

1953–1992

The World Sportscar Championship ran under various names from 1953, with legendary battles between Ferrari, Jaguar, Mercedes-Benz, Maserati, Porsche and Aston Martin. Its golden years included the Porsche 917 versus Ferrari 512 fights of 1970–71 and the Group C era of the 1980s. Rising costs from the 3.5-litre engine rules made manufacturers leave, and the championship ended after 1992.

LM

The Le Mans Series Era

2004–2011

The Le Mans Series keeps endurance racing alive in Europe while the American Le Mans Series carries the flag in the USA. Audi dominates with the R8 and then the R10 TDI, which in 2006 becomes the first diesel car to win Le Mans. Peugeot strikes back with the 908 HDi FAP, starting a diesel war that wins Peugeot Le Mans in 2009.

LMP

The Modern WEC & Hypercar Era

2012–Present

The FIA World Endurance Championship is reborn in 2012 for the LMP1 hybrid era. Audi, Toyota and Porsche field extremely complex hybrid prototypes with around 1,000bhp combined. In 2017 Kamui Kobayashi's Toyota TS050 sets the fastest lap ever on the current Le Mans layout (3:14.791), and in 2018 an unrestricted Porsche 919 Hybrid Evo smashes the Nürburgring Nordschleife lap record. Toyota finally wins Le Mans in 2018 after decades of heartbreak. The Hypercar class arrives in 2021, and by 2024 Ferrari, Porsche, Peugeot, Cadillac and BMW have all joined Toyota at the top.

GT3 Racing &
Gentleman Racers

GT3 represents the golden balance between accessibility and performance. Introduced in 2006, the GT3 class allows wealthy privateers and gentleman drivers to race production-based supercars from Porsche, Ferrari, Audi, Mercedes, BMW and more all balanced through BoP (Balance of Performance) to ensure close, competitive racing.

The Philosophy of GT3

2006 - Present

GT3 was designed as the "customer racing" class: cars that wealthy amateurs and professional drivers could purchase from manufacturers and race competitively. Unlike GT1 or LMP1, GT3 focuses on close racing through Balance of Performance (BoP) regulations that adjust each car's weight, power, and aerodynamics to keep all manufacturers competitive. This creates incredible battles where driver skill matters more than outright speed.

1994
BPR Global GT Series Launched
Stéphane Ratel, Jürgen Barth and Patrick Peter create the BPR series, a pan-European championship for genuine GT supercars. The Ferrari F40, Porsche 911 GT2 and Venturi 600LM headline the grids, and the series becomes one of the most spectacular in the world.
1995
McLaren F1 GTR Conquers Le Mans
The McLaren F1 GTR, a racing version of a road car, dominates the BPR series and then wins Le Mans outright against purpose-built prototypes. JJ Lehto, Yannick Dalmas and Masanori Sekiya win a race with around 17 hours of rain, and McLarens finish 1st, 3rd, 4th and 5th.
1996
Porsche 911 GT1 & The Rules Are Stretched
Porsche arrives with the 911 GT1, a purpose-built racer with only a few road versions made to satisfy homologation. It finishes 2nd overall at Le Mans on its debut. From now on, GT1 cars are race cars first and road cars second.
1997
FIA GT Championship & The Mercedes CLK GTR
The FIA takes over the BPR series and renames it the FIA GT Championship. Mercedes-Benz arrives with the V12 CLK GTR, and McLaren answers with the F1 GTR "Longtail". The two battle all year, and Bernd Schneider wins the title for Mercedes.
1998
Total Mercedes Domination
Mercedes wins all ten FIA GT rounds with the CLK GTR and the new CLK LM, and Klaus Ludwig and Ricardo Zonta take the title. At Le Mans, though, the Porsche 911 GT1-98 finishes 1st and 2nd overall.
1999
The End of GT1
With too few entries, the FIA drops the GT1 class and the Chrysler Viper GTS-R becomes the new top GT car. At Le Mans, Mercedes' CLR prototypes take off at speed, twice with Mark Webber and once with Peter Dumbreck in the race. Mercedes withdraws, and the wildest era of GT racing is over.

Every sport has its own language. Here's a plain-English guide to every term used throughout this website so nothing goes over your head.

Motorsport vocabulary can feel overwhelming at first. These definitions cover the classes, regulations, car types, and race formats mentioned across this site, grouped by category so you can find what you need quickly.

Series & Championships
IMSA

Stands for International Motor Sports Association. It is the governing body and championship series for sports car racing in North America. Think of it as the organisation that organises races like the Daytona 24h and sets the rules all teams must follow.

Series & Championships
Formula 1 (F1)

The FIA Formula 1 World Championship is the pinnacle of single-seater racing and the most prestigious motorsport series in the world. F1 cars are purpose-built, open-wheel racing machines representing the absolute cutting edge of automotive technology. Championships run from March to December with races (called Grands Prix) across five continents.

Series & Championships
IndyCar

The NTT IndyCar Series is North America's top single-seater championship. All teams use the same Dallara chassis with either a Chevrolet or Honda engine, so driver skill and team strategy decide races. It is the only major series that races on ovals, street circuits and road courses in the same season, and its crown jewel is the Indianapolis 500.

Series & Championships
FIA WEC

The FIA World Endurance Championship — the global version of IMSA. Run by the FIA (the worldwide governing body of motorsport), WEC races across multiple continents with Le Mans as its centrepiece event. "World" means it visits several continents, with rounds in Europe, Asia and the Americas.

Series & Championships
BPR Global GT Series

A privately-organised racing series founded in 1994 by Barth, Peter and Ratel (the B, P and R). It gave GT supercars a proper international championship to race in before the FIA took over and renamed it the FIA GT Championship in 1997.

F1 Terms
Grand Prix

French for "Grand Prize" — the name given to each individual F1 race. Each Grand Prix is named after its host country or region (e.g., Monaco Grand Prix, British Grand Prix). A full F1 season consists of approximately 20–24 Grands Prix held worldwide.

F1 Terms
Pole Position

The first starting position on the grid, awarded to the driver who sets the fastest qualifying lap time. Starting from pole gives a significant advantage as the driver leads into the first corner with clear track ahead. Lewis Hamilton holds the all-time record with more than 100 pole positions.

F1 Terms
DRS (Drag Reduction System)

A moveable rear wing flap that reduced drag to give extra speed on straights. From 2011 to 2025, drivers could open it in set zones when within one second of the car ahead, to help overtaking. For 2026 it was replaced by active aerodynamics on every car and an electric "overtake mode" boost.

F1 Terms
Qualifying

A time-trial session held before the race to determine the starting grid order. Qualifying is split into three knockout rounds (Q1, Q2, Q3), with the slowest drivers eliminated after each round. The fastest driver in Q3 earns pole position.

F1 Terms
Constructor

The team/manufacturer that builds the F1 car — examples include Mercedes, Ferrari, Red Bull Racing, McLaren. The Constructors' Championship is awarded to the team that scores the most combined points from both of its drivers across a season. It's considered just as prestigious as the Drivers' Championship.

F1 Terms
Parc Ferme

French for "closed park" — a strict set of regulations that prevents teams from making significant changes to their cars between qualifying and the race. Once a car enters parc fermé (after qualifying), only minor adjustments are allowed. This ensures teams don't run special qualifying setups.

F1 Terms
Power Unit

Modern F1's term for the complete propulsion system — a 1.6-litre turbocharged V6 combined with electric motor-generators that harvest and deploy energy. Since 2026 the MGU-H has been dropped and the electric share has grown to roughly half of the total output, running on fully sustainable fuel. They are the most efficient racing engines ever built.

F1 Terms
Undercut / Overcut

Pit stop strategies used to gain track position. An undercut means pitting earlier than your rival to gain an advantage with fresh tyres on clear track. An overcut is the opposite — staying out longer on older tyres, hoping the extra track time gains more than fresh tyres would.

IndyCar Terms
Indianapolis 500

The 500-mile race (200 laps) held every May at the Indianapolis Motor Speedway since 1911. Known as "The Greatest Spectacle in Racing", it draws more than 300,000 spectators and is part of motorsport's Triple Crown. It is so important that many drivers value winning it more than the IndyCar championship itself.

IndyCar Terms
Oval Racing

Racing on a closed loop of banked left-hand turns, like Indianapolis or Texas. Speeds are extremely high (over 370 km/h at Indy) and cars run nose-to-tail in each other's slipstream for lap after lap. The skills are completely different from road courses: tiny setup changes and bravery in traffic make the difference.

IndyCar Terms
Push-to-Pass

A button that gives a temporary power boost (around 60 extra horsepower) on road and street courses. Each driver gets a fixed number of seconds to use across the whole race, so they must choose between attacking, defending or saving it for the final laps. It is IndyCar's answer to F1's DRS, but it can be used for defending too.

IndyCar Terms
Aeroscreen

A ballistic screen that wraps around the cockpit, introduced in 2020. It combines a titanium frame (similar to F1's Halo) with a toughened polycarbonate windscreen that protects the driver's head from debris. It is especially important on ovals, where parts can fly off at more than 350 km/h.

IndyCar Terms
Rolling Start

Unlike F1's standing starts, IndyCar races usually begin with the field already moving behind the pace car. When the pace car pulls into the pits, the leader controls the pace until the green flag waves. At the Indy 500 the 33 cars line up in eleven rows of three, a tradition unique to the race.

IndyCar Terms
Full-Course Caution

When there is a crash or debris, IndyCar brings out the yellow flag for the whole track and sends out a pace car. The field bunches up behind it and the pit lane usually closes at first. Cautions reset the gaps between cars, which is why IndyCar races so often end with close finishes and dramatic strategy gambles.

IndyCar Terms
Bump Day

The Indy 500 has just 33 starting spots. When more cars enter than that, the slowest qualifiers are "bumped" out of the race in a tense shootout now called Last Chance Qualifying. Even famous names have failed to make the field, including Fernando Alonso with McLaren in 2019.

IndyCar Terms
Drinking the Milk

The Indy 500 winner celebrates by drinking a bottle of milk in Victory Lane instead of spraying champagne. It began when Louis Meyer drank buttermilk after winning in 1936, and it became an official tradition in 1956. Before the race, each driver even tells the organisers whether they would like whole, 2% or skimmed milk.

IndyCar Terms
Triple Crown of Motorsport

The unofficial title for winning the Monaco Grand Prix, the Indianapolis 500 and the 24 Hours of Le Mans during a career. Only Graham Hill has ever achieved it. Fernando Alonso has won Monaco and Le Mans and went to Indianapolis several times to try to complete the set.

Car Classes
GT1 Class

The top class of GT racing in the late 1990s. Cars had to be based on a road-going production vehicle but are heavily modified for racing. GT1 cars produced 600+ horsepower and were the fastest GT cars of their era — think McLaren F1 GTR, Mercedes CLK GTR, Porsche 911 GT1.

Car Classes
GTP Class

Grand Touring Prototype — IMSA's top class through the 1980s and early 90s. Unlike GT classes, GTP cars were purpose-built racing prototypes not derived from road cars. The Porsche 962 and Jaguar XJR-9 are famous GTP cars. They dominated IMSA the way F1 cars dominate their own world.

Car Classes
LMP1 / LMP2

Le Mans Prototype classes. LMP1 was the absolute top class — the fastest, most technologically advanced racing cars in the world during the hybrid era (Audi R18, Porsche 919, Toyota TS050). LMP2 is a spec prototype class one step below, used as a stepping stone for professional drivers.

Car Classes
Hypercar Class

The top class in WEC since 2021, replacing LMP1. Designed to bring more manufacturers back to the top of endurance racing with lower costs and Balance of Performance. Cars are either Le Mans Hypercars (LMH) or LMDh prototypes that can also race in IMSA. Ferrari, Porsche, Toyota, Cadillac, BMW and Peugeot all compete. Think of it as the new era of factory warfare.

Car Classes
GT3 Class

The customer racing class introduced in 2006. GT3 cars are production-based supercars (Porsche 911, Ferrari 488, Audi R8, Mercedes-AMG GT3) modified for racing and sold to private teams. All GT3 cars are balanced through BoP (Balance of Performance) to ensure close racing regardless of manufacturer. GT3 is the most popular GT class worldwide.

Car Classes
GT4 Class

A more affordable customer racing class below GT3, introduced in 2007. GT4 cars have less power (around 400hp) and fewer modifications than GT3, making them perfect for amateur racers and developing drivers. Think of GT4 as the entry point to professional GT racing.

GT3 Terms
Balance of Performance (BoP)

The equalization system that keeps all GT3 manufacturers competitive. Race organizers adjust each car's weight, engine power, fuel capacity, and aerodynamics to ensure no single manufacturer has an unfair advantage. BoP is updated throughout the season based on race results, creating the closest racing in motorsport.

GT3 Terms
Pro-Am / Silver Cup

A driver category system in GT3 racing. Professional factory drivers are rated Platinum or Gold. Amateur gentlemen racers are rated Silver or Bronze. Pro-Am classes require one professional and one amateur per car, creating opportunities for wealthy privateers to race alongside the world's best drivers.

GT3 Terms
Gentleman Driver

A wealthy amateur racer who purchases a GT3 car and hires professional drivers as teammates. Gentleman drivers fund their own racing programs and compete in Pro-Am classes. Some are genuinely fast (despite their amateur rating), while others simply love racing and have the budget to live the dream.

GT3 Terms
24h Series / GT World Challenge

The major GT3 championship series worldwide. The GT World Challenge (formerly Blancpain GT Series) crowns European, Asian, and American champions. The 24H Series runs endurance races at circuits like Spa, Nurburgring, and Dubai. These championships are where GT3's best drivers and teams compete for glory.

Race Formats
Sprint Race

A short-distance race typically lasting 60-90 minutes, where outright speed matters more than tyre management or fuel strategy. F1 races are sprint format — drivers push flat-out for the entire duration. Opposite of endurance racing.

Race Formats
Endurance Racing

Races measured in hours rather than laps. The winner is the car that covers the most distance within the time limit. Drivers must manage tyre wear, fuel consumption and mechanical stress over an entire day (or night). Strategy, reliability and driver changes are just as important as outright speed.

Race Formats
Multi-Class Racing

When different categories of cars race simultaneously on the same track. At Le Mans, LMP1 prototypes, LMP2 prototypes and GT cars all race at the same time. Each class has its own winner, but all cars share the same asphalt, which means a slower GT car and a much faster prototype must navigate around each other safely.

Technical Terms
Downforce

Aerodynamic force that pushes the car downward onto the track, generated by wings, splitters and the car's underbody. More downforce = more grip through corners = faster cornering speeds. The trade-off is drag, which reduces top speed on straights. Racing cars spend enormous effort finding the right balance between the two.

Technical Terms
Homologation

The official approval process that makes a racing car legal to compete. For GT1, a manufacturer had to build a minimum number of road-going versions of the car before the race version was approved. This is why the McLaren F1 GTR, CLK GTR and Porsche 911 GT1 all have road car counterparts — they had to exist to satisfy homologation rules.

Technical Terms
Hybrid Power

A system where a car uses both a traditional combustion engine and an electric motor. In LMP1 racing, cars like the Porsche 919 and Toyota TS050 used hybrid systems that recovered energy under braking and deployed it as extra power out of corners. These systems produced over 1,000 combined horsepower and made the cars devastatingly fast.

Famous Circuits
Monaco

The Monaco Grand Prix — F1's most prestigious and glamorous race, held on the tight, narrow streets of Monte Carlo. Overtaking is nearly impossible, making qualifying crucial. Winning Monaco is considered the ultimate achievement in F1. Ayrton Senna won here six times, more than any other driver.

Famous Circuits
Spa-Francorchamps

The legendary Circuit de Spa-Francorchamps in Belgium — one of the most challenging and revered tracks in motorsport. Famous for Eau Rouge, a high-speed uphill corner that tests driver bravery. At 7km long, Spa rewards commitment, car balance, and fearlessness.

Famous Circuits
La Sarthe

The official name of the Le Mans circuit — Circuit de la Sarthe is named after the Sarthe departement (region) in northwest France where it's located. It's a semi-permanent circuit, meaning most of it uses public roads that are closed off for race week, with only a small purpose-built section. It measures 13.626 km per lap.

Famous Circuits
The Brickyard

The nickname of the Indianapolis Motor Speedway. In 1909 the original track was paved with 3.2 million bricks. Today it is asphalt apart from a one-yard strip of the original bricks at the start/finish line, called the "Yard of Bricks". Winning drivers traditionally kneel down and kiss it. The 4.023 km oval is the world's highest-capacity sporting venue.

Timing & Sectors
Purple Sector

A purple sector time means that lap segment is the fastest anyone has set all session — the absolute best time across every driver. You'll see it on timing screens and TV overlays. Only one driver can hold the purple at any given moment, making it the benchmark everyone is chasing.

Timing & Sectors
Red Sector

A red sector means the lap or sector time has been invalidated — most commonly because the driver exceeded track limits by going beyond the white lines at a corner. Any lap with a red sector is deleted from the timing sheets and does not count toward qualifying or fastest lap records.

Timing & Sectors
Green Sector

A green sector means the driver has beaten their own personal best time through that section of the lap. It doesn't mean they're the fastest overall (that's purple), but they are improving on themselves — a good sign they're extracting more from the car or conditions are getting better.

Timing & Sectors
Yellow Sector

A yellow sector means the driver's time through that part of the lap is slower than their own personal best for that sector. It's a sign they lost time there compared to their reference lap — whether from a mistake, traffic, tyre degradation, or a cautious approach through a corner.

Racing Flags
🏁 Chequered Flag

The most iconic flag in motorsport. Waved at the finish line to signal the end of a race, qualifying session, or practice. When the leader sees the chequered flag, the race is over. All other drivers complete their current lap and the results are declared official.

Racing Flags
🟡 Yellow Flag

Signals danger ahead — usually a stopped car, debris, or an incident on track. Drivers must slow down and are strictly forbidden from overtaking under yellow. A double yellow (two flags waved together) means be prepared to stop. Ignoring a yellow flag results in a time penalty or disqualification.

Racing Flags
🔴 Red Flag

The session is stopped immediately. All drivers must slow down and return to the pit lane. Red flags are shown when conditions are too dangerous to continue — a serious accident, heavy rain making the track undriveable, or debris that requires clearing. Races can be restarted once the issue is resolved.

Racing Flags
🔵 Blue Flag

Shown to a driver about to be lapped by a faster car. It's a warning to let the leading car past without impeding it. In F1, a driver shown three blue flags without moving aside faces a penalty. Blue flags are one of the most debated topics in motorsport — especially when backmarkers hold up title contenders.

Racing Flags
🟢 Green Flag

Signals that the track is clear and racing can resume — typically shown after a yellow flag period, safety car, or at the start of a session. When drivers see the green flag, full racing speed and overtaking are permitted again. At race starts it's also used to release cars from the pit lane.

Racing Flags
🟠 Orange Flag (Meatball)

Also known as the meatball flag — a black flag with an orange disc in the centre. It's shown alongside a car number to tell that specific driver they have a mechanical problem and must pit immediately for repairs. The name comes from the circular orange shape resembling a meatball.

Racing Flags
⬛ Black Flag

The most severe flag shown to an individual driver — it means disqualification. Shown alongside the car number, it orders that driver to return to the pits immediately and retire from the race. Reasons include dangerous driving, ignoring penalties, or a serious rules violation. Once black-flagged, the result stands and cannot be appealed on the day.

Racing Flags
⬜ White Flag

The white flag has two different meanings depending on the series. In Formula 1, it warns drivers that a slow-moving staff or medical vehicle is on track — drivers must be alert and prepared to avoid it. In IMSA and other endurance/sportscar series, it signals the final lap of the race, letting drivers know the leader has crossed the line to begin their last tour of the circuit.
